What the game is actually about

The balance is the whole game

Every other survival game asks how fast you can strip a place. This one asks what is left when you are finished — because the valley answers back, on a delay long enough that you will have forgotten what you did.

Clear the slope of trees and you have a winter of firewood and a field. You have also taken away the sponge that held the rain. The next storm runs straight off the bare ground, strips the soil you meant to farm, and drops it as silt in the river. The silt fills the pool behind your dam and chokes the weir the fish run through. None of it is a scripted punishment. It is four systems doing what they honestly do, in the order they do it.

  • You cutA slope of timber, felled for the woodpile and a clear field.
  • The rain runsNothing holds it now. It carries the topsoil downhill.
  • The river siltsYour soil settles behind the dam and in the weir.
  • The fish stopAnd the field you cleared it for is thinner every year.
Build so the valley is richer for your being in it, not poorer. Every decision is weighed against the seventh generation — the people who will live here long after you are a name in the chronicle.

Nine valleys, and none of them forgives the same mistake

Every ground has its own argument

A boreal taiga, a Chihuahuan scrub, a rice-river valley, a high Andean shelf, the Sahel's edge, a Pacific rain coast, an Apennine hillside, a Baltic shore. Each carries its own weather, its own plants and its own beasts — and its own way of killing you. What you learn in one is half-wrong in the next.

Five ways of looking at one valley

From open sky to a measured house

You start with nothing at all

Not a starter hut. Open ground, and a night coming. Pile branches into a debris hut and the ladder begins: mud into the weave, reed over it, stones at the foot. Every rung you raise is redrawn as a measured sheet — plan, section, elevation — so you can see exactly what you have built and what it is keeping out.

The ages

Two hundred and twenty-one things to make, and the reasons between them

Every craft in the game is a real one, in the order history actually found it. You cannot fire brick without a kiln, cannot melt copper without forced air, cannot force air without something to force it with.

A hundred and nine of them are STRUCTURES — things that stand on the ground and stay there. A fire pit, a drying rack, a smoke house, a well, a kiln, a byre, a wall, a gate, a forge. They are sited on the plan, they take damage, they are repaired and upgraded, and every one of them changes the arithmetic of a night.

Nothing unlocks on a timer. Things become possible because you made the thing before them.

Three rules the whole game obeys

  1. Everything you see is true.What a person wears says what you have made. Black earth on the plan is black earth in the world. Nothing is decoration.
  2. The valley is cast once, and then it is real.Plenty happens that you cannot predict — a heat dome settles for a fortnight, the salmon run comes early or late, a mast year buries the ground in acorns. But none of it is a coin flipped behind your back: every one of those was decided by the valley's own seed before you arrived, and the same valley lived the same way replays exactly. Nothing rerolls because you reloaded.
  3. Nothing is free.Every hour spent is an hour not spent elsewhere, and every yield taken is taken from something that has to recover.

The first of three

Survive, then Thrive, then Refine

One valley is a long story, and it does not all belong in one game. Survive is the whole of it that a household can do alone: fire, food, a roof, the first winters, and the slow argument with the ground that decides whether your grandchildren inherit a place or a ruin. It is finished, and it is what you can buy.

The two that follow are planned, not promised — and each is a game, not an expansion pack.

  1. Book I

    Survive

    Stone, clay and the homestead. One keeper, then a family, and everything a pair of hands can make from what a valley actually contains. Out now.

  2. Book II

    Thrive

    The hamlet and the village. Neighbours who did not choose you, roofs that are not yours, a road that brings strangers, and the things a settlement can make that no household can — because they need more hands than one house has.

  3. Book III

    Refine

    Metal at scale, and the crafts that need a whole society standing behind them: people who do one thing all year, and a valley asked to carry far more than it ever has.
